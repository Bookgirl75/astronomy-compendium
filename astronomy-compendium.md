## Light - the First Messenger

### The Electromagnetic Spectrum

We first observed stars and other bodies using visible light.
Babylonian astronomers got systematic in their observations and built
knowledge bases and could predict ... using visible light.

In 1864, Maxwell's theory
of electromagnetism proposed that electric and magnetic fields interplay, creating waves. His equations united radio, heat, and visible light in one spectrum. But it wasn't until Hertz in 1887 that Maxwell's experiments were truly proven correct, through experimentation. 

In the late 19th and early 20th century we discovered x-rays and
gamma-rays, also part of the same spectrum but with very short
wavelength (i.e. very high frequency) compared to visible light.

Today we speak of the "electromagnetic spectrum" [insert picture from
wikipedia] which goes from the longest wavelength radio waves
(1,000 meters) to sub-picometer (10^-12 meters) wavelength gamma rays

But what qualifies something as electromagnetic? Well, electromagnetic waves are waves that don't need a medium like air or water to travel. They have no mass because they are made of pure energy. They occur when electric and magnetic fields oscillate perpendicular to each other. 

###But before we dive in, some jargon:

optical, frequency, wavelength, speed

Wavelength: the distance between two peaks in a wave [insert image]. Represented by the Greek letter Lambda, and can be measured in picometers, nanometers, centimeters, meters, or kilometers
Frequency: The amount of wave cycles in a second. Represented by the Greek letter nu and measured in Hertz (Hz). 
Frequency and wavelength are inversely related and commonly represented by the equation [insert speed of light = frequency*wavelength] in which the speed of light (c) is in m/s, wavelength (lambda) is in m, and frequency (nu) is in Hz 

        1. Radio - Radio waves have the largest wavelength, ranging from 100km to 1mm. Because of the large wavelength, they have the lowest frequency, ranging from 3Hz to 10^9Hz (1GHz). Radio waves are used by humans for all sorts of communication. You can find them utilized in TVs, satellites, telephones, and more! These waves travel in straight lines and can penetrate nonconducting materials but cannot pass through electrical conductors. 
        2. Microwaves - Microwaves have the second largest wavelength, ranging from 30 cm to 1 mm, with frequencies of 1,000 to 300,000 MHz. Microwaves are used most often for high-speed data transitions and in cooking or reheating food. When it comes to astrophysics, microwaves are most often mentioned in terms of the Cosmic Microwave Background, which will be discussed later in the chapter. 
        3. Infrared - Nestled right next to the red end of the visible light spectrum, infrared radiation has wavelengths ranging from 0.1 to 7.5 * 10^-5 cm, with frequencies of 10^12 to 5 * 10^14 Hz. Infrared radiation is strongly absorbed by water and the atmosphere, and makes up almost 50% of the sun's emitted radiation. 
        4. Visible - The light in which we see the world. In humans, we generally see light through three cones in our eyes, one that responses to long wavelengths, one for medium, and one for short. Whatever wavelengths of visible light are absorbed by an object are what we don't see and what is reflected back into our eyes is what is seen. The visible light spectrum is also the basis for spectroscopy, which will be discussed later in the chapter. 
        5. UV - UV, or Ultraviolet light, consists of wavelengths between 100 to 400 nanometers, and frequencies between 7.5 * 10^14 Hz to 3 * 10^16 Hz. UV radiation is accounts for 10% of the sun's radiation, and There are three main types of UV light, UV A, B, and C. Ultraviolet A is long-wave radiation, it is not absorbed by the ozone layer, and does little harm to the human body. These are the uv rays closest to visible light. UV B radiation is mostly absorbed by the ozone layer but what isn't can cause sunburns and skin cancer. UV C is the highest frequency, and is very harmful to living creatures. Luckily, they are unable to penetrate the ozone layer. These rays are also created artificially and used to sterilize equipment or purify water.
